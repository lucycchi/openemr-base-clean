# Evaluation of anemia in adults in primary care (summary)

## Definition
Anemia is a hemoglobin below the laboratory reference range, commonly below 13.5 g/dL in men and below 12 g/dL in nonpregnant women (WHO thresholds 13 and 12 g/dL). A single mild low value in a well patient is repeated before extensive workup; a new or worsening anemia, or one with symptoms, is evaluated promptly.

## First step: mean corpuscular volume
Classify by mean corpuscular volume (MCV). Microcytic anemia (MCV below 80 fL) is most often iron deficiency, then thalassemia trait and anemia of chronic disease. Normocytic anemia (80 to 100 fL) includes anemia of chronic disease, chronic kidney disease, early iron deficiency, hemolysis and marrow disorders. Macrocytic anemia (above 100 fL) suggests vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, certain medications or myelodysplasia.

## Iron deficiency
Ferritin is the most useful single test: a ferritin below 30 ng/mL indicates iron deficiency; ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, so values up to 100 ng/mL do not exclude deficiency when inflammation or CKD is present, and transferrin saturation below 20 percent supports the diagnosis. Iron deficiency in a man or a postmenopausal woman warrants evaluation of the gastrointestinal tract for blood loss unless another cause is clear. Oral iron is first-line; recheck hemoglobin in 2 to 4 weeks and expect a rise of about 1 g/dL.

## B12 and folate
Vitamin B12 below 200 pg/mL is deficient; 200 to 300 pg/mL is borderline and methylmalonic acid confirms deficiency. Replace B12 orally or by injection and recheck the count in 4 to 8 weeks. Check folate when macrocytosis persists.

## When to refer
Refer to hematology for anemia with abnormal white cell or platelet counts, suspected hemolysis, unexplained macrocytosis, failure to respond to replacement, or a hemoglobin below 8 g/dL without an obvious reversible cause; refer to gastroenterology for iron deficiency needing endoscopic evaluation.
